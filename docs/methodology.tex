\begin{abstract}
This document is the mathematical reference for \texttt{cryptorisk}, a study that
asks a single question: \emph{of the standard families of volatility and
tail-risk models, which one produces the best one-day-ahead Value-at-Risk and
Expected Shortfall for a BTC/ETH position, measured strictly out-of-sample and
with a defensible notion of ``best''?} We motivate the problem and the design
(\S\ref{sec:intro}); fix the probabilistic setup, the risk functionals and the
walk-forward protocol (\S\ref{sec:setup}); describe the data, including the
5-minute series that makes realized measures possible (\S\ref{sec:data}); derive
the realized measures (\S\ref{sec:realized}); specify each of the sixteen models
--- mean, variance, innovation law, stationarity/identification conditions, the
estimation objective, and the closed-form VaR/ES (\S\ref{sec:models}); and give
the full evaluation battery: coverage and independence tests, the Dynamic
Quantile test, Expected-Shortfall backtests, strictly consistent scoring
functions, the Diebold--Mariano test, the Model Confidence Set, and the
Berkowitz density test (\S\ref{sec:eval}). Two closing sections cover the
decision layer (\S\ref{sec:decision}) and three limitations that the data
imposes and the methodology exposes (\S\ref{sec:limits}). Proofs are in the
cited literature; the emphasis here is on writing every object down explicitly
and saying, in words, what it is for.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The problem}

A desk holding a spot position in Bitcoin and Ether needs a daily answer to:
\emph{how much could this position lose tomorrow, and how bad is the loss if it
goes past that?} The first quantity is Value-at-Risk (VaR), the second is
Expected Shortfall (ES). They feed a capital number, a position-limit framework,
and --- when the model is wrong in a way that matters --- a decision to hedge.
The forecast is only as good as the model that produces it, and crypto is an
unusually hostile environment for the textbook models.

\subsection{Why crypto returns are hard}\label{sec:stylized}

Daily log-returns of BTC and ETH over 2018--2026 exhibit, in ascending order of
inconvenience:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Heavy tails.} The sample excess kurtosis is an order of magnitude
      above the Gaussian value of zero; the $1\%$ empirical quantile is roughly
      $2.4$ conditional standard deviations out, versus $2.33$ for a normal.
\item \textbf{Volatility clustering.} $|r_t|$ and $r_t^2$ are strongly
      autocorrelated over months; the ARCH-LM test rejects homoskedasticity
      overwhelmingly. This is what makes conditional models (GARCH and
      descendants) worth the trouble.
\item \textbf{Asymmetry.} Large negative returns raise next-day volatility more
      than large positive ones, though less sharply than in equity indices, and
      occasionally the sign reverses (a violent rally is itself
      destabilising).
\item \textbf{Jumps.} Some single-day moves --- an exchange failure, a
      stablecoin de-peg, a forced-deleveraging cascade --- are better described
      as discrete events than as large draws from a continuous law. Bipower
      variation and the Barndorff-Nielsen--Shephard test locate them.
\item \textbf{Regime shifts.} The series looks like it alternates between a calm
      state and a turbulent one, motivating Markov-switching specifications ---
      though, as \S\ref{sec:limits} shows, the state is only weakly identified
      from short windows.
\item \textbf{Microstructure and reference-price issues.} 24/7 trading (no
      overnight gap), venue fragmentation, and in 2018 a Tether/USD basis of up
      to $8\%$ that contaminates any single-exchange price. The study uses a
      multi-venue USD reference rate as the canonical series.
\end{enumerate}

No single model captures all six. The point of the study is to let them compete
on identical data and an identical protocol, and to report which ones survive.

\subsection{What ``good'' means}

Three properties organise the evaluation.

\paragraph{Coherence.} ES is a coherent risk measure (monotone, sub-additive,
positively homogeneous, translation-invariant); VaR is not (it can fail
sub-additivity). This is one reason the regulatory frame (Basel III / FRTB) has
moved to ES at $97.5\%$. We report both because VaR is still the object every
backtesting test was built for.

\paragraph{Elicitability and backtestability.} A functional $T$ of a
distribution is \emph{elicitable} if there is a loss $S(x,y)$ whose expected
value is minimised at $x = T(F)$ when $y\sim F$. VaR is elicitable (the pinball
loss). ES \emph{alone} is not (Gneiting, 2011). But the \emph{pair}
$(\VaR_\alpha,\ES_\alpha)$ is jointly elicitable (Fissler \& Ziegel, 2016), and
that joint scoring function --- FZ0 in \S\ref{sec:fz} --- is what lets us
\emph{rank} models rather than merely pass/fail them.

\paragraph{Out-of-sample discipline.} Every number is produced by a strict
walk-forward: at each date the model sees only the past, and the train/test
split is frozen before any result is inspected. This is the stance of an
independent model-validation function, not of a modeller tuning to a backtest.

\subsection{Relation to v1}

A first version (project \texttt{cubo-btc-risk}) built five models and a
dashboard. Its lessons, carried into v2:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item the GARCH VaR was inflated by $\sqrt{\nu/(\nu-2)}$ because the Student-$t$
      quantile was not rescaled to unit variance (\S\ref{sec:garch});
\item the rolling-window MS-GARCH regime probability had no out-of-sample
      signal --- a genuine identification result, not a bug (\S\ref{sec:limits});
\item Monte-Carlo scenario ``fan charts'' were not a substitute for a proper
      backtest.
\end{itemize}
v2 replaces the dashboard-as-deliverable with a comparative study: sixteen
models, a real data layer, and a battery that ranks with statistical
significance.

\subsection{What the reader gets from this document}

Section~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:realized} fix notation and the observables.
Section~\ref{sec:models} is a catalogue: every model's spec, likelihood, and
VaR/ES formula. Section~\ref{sec:eval} is the evaluation methodology, with the
statistic of every test written out and, for the two hardest objects (the FZ0
scoring function and the Model Confidence Set), the underlying theory. The
running example throughout is a single asset at a single $\alpha$; the study
repeats everything over $\{$BTC, ETH$\} \times \{0.025, 0.01\}$ and reports it as
tables.

\paragraph{How to read it.} The document is meant to be usable at two depths.
For the argument alone, read this introduction, the italic \emph{In one line}
opener under each model, the \emph{Reading} paragraph that closes most
subsections, and Table~\ref{tab:tests} (every evaluation test on one page). For

\section{Setup and the forecasting problem}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Probability space and returns}

Fix a probability space with a filtration $\{\Fcal_t\}_{t\ge0}$ representing the
information available at the end of day $t$. Let $P_t$ be the daily close and
\begin{equation}
  r_t \eqdef \ln P_t - \ln P_{t-1}
\end{equation}
the log-return. We assume $\{r_t\}$ is $\Fcal_t$-adapted and, for the
conditional models, that there is a well-defined one-step predictive law
$F_t(\cdot) \eqdef \Prob(r_{t+1}\le \cdot \mid \Fcal_t)$ with density $f_t$ where
it exists.

\subsection{Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall}

\begin{definition}[Risk functionals]
For $\alpha\in(0,\tfrac12)$,
\begin{align}
  \VaR_{\alpha,t}
    &\eqdef \inf\{x\in\R : F_t(x) \ge \alpha\}
     = F_t^{-1}(\alpha), \\
  \ES_{\alpha,t}
    &\eqdef \frac{1}{\alpha}\int_0^\alpha F_t^{-1}(p)\,dp
     = \E\!\left[\,r_{t+1}\,\middle|\, r_{t+1}\le \VaR_{\alpha,t},\ \Fcal_t\right]
       \quad (\text{if } F_t \text{ is continuous at } \VaR_{\alpha,t}).
\end{align}
\end{definition}
Both are negative return levels for the $\alpha$ we use. Note $\ES_{\alpha,t}\le
\VaR_{\alpha,t} < 0$ always, and $\ES$ is the average of the worst $\alpha$-fraction
of outcomes, so it ``sees'' the shape of the tail beyond the quantile.

\intuition{If tomorrow's return is Gaussian with mean $0$ and s.d.\ $3\%$, then
$\VaR_{0.025}= 0.03\,\Phi^{-1}(0.025)= -5.9\%$ (a worse-than-$5.9\%$ loss has
$2.5\%$ probability) and $\ES_{0.025}= -0.03\,\phi(\Phi^{-1}(0.025))/0.025 =
-7.0\%$ (the average loss \emph{given} you are past the VaR). Fat tails push the
two further apart; every model below is a different way of guessing tomorrow's
shape.}

\paragraph{The violation process.} With
$I_{t+1}\eqdef\Ind{r_{t+1} < \VaR_{\alpha,t}}$, a correctly specified model
satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \E[I_{t+1}\mid\Fcal_t] = \alpha
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \{I_t - \alpha\}_{t} \text{ is a martingale difference sequence.}
  \label{eq:mds}
\end{equation}
Property \eqref{eq:mds} is the null hypothesis of every VaR backtest in
\S\ref{sec:eval}: not only should the unconditional breach rate be $\alpha$
(Kupiec), the breaches should be unpredictable from anything in $\Fcal_t$ ---
past breaches (Christoffersen) or the VaR level itself (Dynamic Quantile).

\subsection{Predictive-distribution representations}

Each model emits one of three objects per day.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Location--scale.} $r_{t+1} = \mu_{t+1} + \sigma_{t+1}\,Z$ with $Z$
      from a fixed standardized law $G$ (mean $0$, variance $1$ where it
      exists). Then $\VaR_{\alpha,t} = \mu_{t+1} + \sigma_{t+1}\,G^{-1}(\alpha)$
      and $\ES_{\alpha,t} = \mu_{t+1} + \sigma_{t+1}\,\bar G(\alpha)$ with
      $\bar G(\alpha)\eqdef \alpha^{-1}\int_0^\alpha G^{-1}(p)\,dp$.
\item \textbf{Sample-based.} $F_t$ is the (possibly weighted) empirical
      distribution of a sample $\{z_s\}$, optionally shifted and scaled;
      $\VaR$ and $\ES$ are its weighted quantile and tail mean.
\item \textbf{Quantile-only.} The model returns the pair
      $(\VaR_{\alpha,t},\ES_{\alpha,t})$ for the configured $\alpha$'s directly,
      with no full $F_t$ (CAViaR; the MS-GARCH bridge). No PIT is available.
\end{itemize}
Whenever $F_t$ is available we also record the \emph{probability integral
transform} $u_{t+1}\eqdef F_t(r_{t+1})$; under a correct model
$\{u_t\}\stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathrm{U}(0,1)$ (Rosenblatt, 1952).

\subsection{The walk-forward protocol}\label{sec:wf}

Let the full sample be $t=1,\dots,T$ and fix an out-of-sample start $t_0$. For
$t = t_0,\dots,T$:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item form $W_t = \{t-w,\dots,t-1\}$ (rolling, fixed $w=500$) or
      $W_t=\{1,\dots,t-1\}$ (expanding); both are run and reported;
\item if $t \equiv t_0 \pmod{k}$ re-estimate parameters on $W_t$ (with
      $k = 1$ for the fast models, $k = 20$ for MS-GARCH); on the intervening
      days keep the parameters but re-filter the latent state on the new
      observations;
\item form $F_t$; record $\VaR_{\alpha,t}$, $\ES_{\alpha,t}$, $\sigma_{t+1}^2$,
      the realized $r_{t+1}$, $I_{t+1}$, and $u_{t+1}$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{assumption}[No look-ahead]
Step 2--3 use only $\Fcal_t$-measurable quantities and only the data vintage
available at $t$. The split $t_0$ is chosen once, before any result is seen.
\end{assumption}

$t_0$ is $2019$-$05$-$16$: the first date with a full $500$-day window given the
sample start of $2018$-$01$-$01$. The out-of-sample period is $\approx 2{,}674$
days per asset.

\subsection{Scoring preliminaries}

\begin{definition}[Consistency]
A scoring function $S:\R^d\times\R\to\R$ is \emph{consistent} for a functional
$T$ relative to a class $\mathcal P$ if
$\E_{Y\sim F}[S(T(F),Y)] \le \E_{Y\sim F}[S(x,Y)]$ for all $F\in\mathcal P$ and
all $x$; \emph{strictly} consistent if equality implies $x = T(F)$.
\end{definition}
Strict consistency is what makes an average score a legitimate ranking: the only
way to minimise it in expectation is to report the true functional value.
Section~\ref{sec:fz} gives the strictly consistent score for
$(\VaR_\alpha,\ES_\alpha)$.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Daily prices}

Two methodologically independent sources are reconciled:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Binance} spot klines (\texttt{BTCUSDT}, \texttt{ETHUSDT}) --- one
      venue's OHLCV;
\item \textbf{CoinMetrics} community \texttt{PriceUSD} --- a multi-venue USD
      \emph{reference rate}, independent of any single exchange.
\end{itemize}
The canonical return series uses the CoinMetrics reference close (falling back
to Binance where CM is missing); both are stored. A day is flagged when the two
disagree in log terms by more than $2\%$ \emph{and} by more than that day's own
return --- the second condition removes timing artefacts (an exchange close and
a reference snapshot differ a few percent on a big-move day). Every stored
price row carries a \texttt{vintage\_ts}, so a backtest as of $t$ can be made to
read only the vintage available at $t$ (point-in-time).

\subsection{Intraday}

$5$-minute bars for both pairs, $\approx 1.8$ million rows, from
\texttt{data.binance.vision}. High-frequency cleaning follows
Barndorff-Nielsen, Hansen, Lunde \& Shephard (2009): drop duplicate timestamps,
non-positive prices, and $5$-minute returns that are MAD-outliers or exceed
$50\%$. These bars exist only to compute the realized measures of
\S\ref{sec:realized}.

\subsection{Context and market-structure blocks}

Descriptive only (never a VaR feature): hashrate and difficulty
(\texttt{blockchain.info}); S\&P~500 and the dollar index (Yahoo). A separate
market-structure block --- perpetual funding, open interest --- is the
exogenous driver for the ``-X'' models; its history is partial, and the -X
models degrade to ``no exogenous term'' when it is absent.

\subsection{Quality gate}

After each ingest a battery runs (\texttt{calendar\_gap},
\texttt{extreme\_return} $|r|>0.40$, \texttt{source\_divergence},
\texttt{few\_intraday\_bars}, \texttt{stale\_price}); known events are explained
in an allow-list (the 2018 USDT/USD basis; Binance outage days; the
2020-03-12 COVID crash), and the pipeline refuses to proceed on any
\emph{unexplained} flag.

\intuition{One clean daily return series that no single exchange can distort, a
separate pile of 5-minute bars used only to measure yesterday's variance, and a
gate that stops the study if it sees a data anomaly it cannot name. Everything
downstream assumes this layer is trustworthy.}

\section{Realized measures}\label{sec:realized}

\inbrief{From the 5-minute bars, build four daily numbers --- how much the price
actually moved ($\RV$), how much of that was smooth rather than jumps ($\BV$),
how much was downside ($\RV^{-}$), and how noisy the $\RV$ estimate itself was
($\RQ$). The realized-measure models in \S\ref{sec:models} are built on these.}

\subsection{Framework}

The formal setup, needed only to state what each measure converges to. Assume
the log-price follows a Brownian semimartingale with finite-activity jumps,
\begin{equation}
  d\ln P_s = \mu_s\,ds + \sigma_s\,dW_s + dJ_s,
  \qquad J_s = \sum_{i} \kappa_i\,\Ind{\tau_i \le s},
\end{equation}
so that the \emph{quadratic variation} over day $t$ splits into a continuous and
a jump part,
\begin{equation}
  \QV_t = \underbrace{\int_{t-1}^{t}\sigma_s^2\,ds}_{\IV_t}
        + \sum_{\tau_i\in(t-1,t]} \kappa_i^2 .
\end{equation}
Let $r_{t,1},\dots,r_{t,M}$ be the $M$ intraday ($5$-minute) log-returns of
day $t$; $M\approx 288$.

\subsection{Realized variance}

\begin{equation}
  \RV_t = \sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^2 \;\pto\; \QV_t
  \qquad (M\to\infty,\ \text{no noise}).
\end{equation}
With microstructure noise, $\RV_t$ at very fine sampling is biased; the standard
compromise is $5$-minute sampling (``sparse'' RV). We additionally report a
subsampled estimator: for a coarsening factor $q$, form $q$-step block sums
starting at each of $q$ offsets, take each grid's RV, and average. This trades a
little bias for a variance reduction (Zhou, 1996).

\subsection{Bipower variation and the jump test}

With $\mu_1 = \E|Z| = \sqrt{2/\pi}$ for $Z\sim N(0,1)$,
\begin{equation}
  \BV_t = \mu_1^{-2}\,\frac{M}{M-1}\sum_{i=2}^{M}|r_{t,i}|\,|r_{t,i-1}|
        \;\pto\; \IV_t .
\end{equation}
$\BV_t$ estimates the \emph{continuous} part only: a lone jump inflates one
$|r_{t,i}|$ but is multiplied by a neighbouring $O_p(M^{-1/2})$ term, so its
contribution vanishes. Hence $\RV_t-\BV_t \ge 0$ is a consistent estimator of
the jump variation. Barndorff-Nielsen \& Shephard (2006) give the CLT that
turns this into a test: under the null of no jumps on day $t$,
\begin{equation}
  Z_t = \frac{(\RV_t-\BV_t)/\RV_t}
             {\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{\pi^2}{4}+\pi-5\right)\dfrac1M
              \max\!\big(1,\ \TQ_t/\BV_t^2\big)}}
        \;\dto\; N(0,1),
\end{equation}
where the \emph{tripower quarticity} (a jump-robust estimator of
$\int\sigma_s^4\,ds$) is
\begin{equation}
  \TQ_t = M\,\mu_{4/3}^{-3}\sum_{i=3}^{M}
          |r_{t,i}|^{4/3}|r_{t,i-1}|^{4/3}|r_{t,i-2}|^{4/3},
  \qquad
  \mu_{4/3} = 2^{2/3}\frac{\Gamma(7/6)}{\Gamma(1/2)}.
\end{equation}
We set the daily jump component to
$\text{jump}_t = (\RV_t-\BV_t)\,\Ind{Z_t > \Phi^{-1}(0.95)}$, i.e.\ zero unless
the jump is significant at $5\%$. The alternative detector (Lee \& Mykland,
2008) standardises each $r_{t,i}$ by a local bipower volatility and compares
$\max_i |r_{t,i}|/\hat\sigma_{t,i}$ to a Gumbel critical value.

\subsection{Realized semivariance}

\begin{equation}
  \RV^{+}_t = \sum_i r_{t,i}^2\Ind{r_{t,i}>0},
  \qquad
  \RV^{-}_t = \sum_i r_{t,i}^2\Ind{r_{t,i}<0},
  \qquad
  \RV^{+}_t + \RV^{-}_t = \RV_t .
\end{equation}
Barndorff-Nielsen, Kinnebrock \& Shephard (2010): $\RV^{-}_t\pto\tfrac12\IV_t +
\sum(\kappa_i^{-})^2$, so the downside semivariance carries the
downside-relevant information and predicts future volatility better than
$\RV_t$.

\subsection{Realized quarticity}

\begin{equation}
  \RQ_t = \frac{M}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^4 \;\pto\; \int_{t-1}^{t}\sigma_s^4\,ds .
\end{equation}
$\RQ_t$ is (proportional to) the asymptotic variance of $\RV_t$:
$\sqrt{M}(\RV_t - \IV_t)\dto N(0,\,2\int\sigma_s^4)$. HARQ uses $\RQ_t^{1/2}$ to
scale down the daily term on days when $\RV_t$ was itself an imprecise estimate
(\S\ref{sec:har}).

\intuition{Intraday data lets us \emph{observe} yesterday's variance rather than
proxy it by one squared return. $\RV$ is the observation, $\BV$ removes the
jumps, $\RV^{-}$ keeps the bad half, and $\RQ$ measures how noisy the
observation was.}

\section{The forecasting models}\label{sec:models}

Family & Models & Central idea \\

Each subsection gives: the specification, the stationarity or identification
conditions, the estimation objective, and the one-step $\VaR_{\alpha,t}$,
$\ES_{\alpha,t}$. Fallbacks (used when an optimiser fails or a forecast
degenerates) are noted; they always reduce to an empirical quantile of the
window.

The order is roughly increasing structure. The first two models make no
distributional assumption at all; EWMA and the GARCH family add a variance
recursion; FHS and GARCH-EVT keep that recursion but treat the tail
non-parametrically; the Merton model adds discrete jumps; the realized-measure
models replace the squared-return signal with observed $\RV$; the last group
either adds an exogenous driver or models the quantile directly. Reading just
the \emph{In one line} opener and the \emph{Reading} close of each subsection
gives the full roster without the algebra.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Historical Simulation and age-weighting}

\inbrief{Tomorrow looks like a random draw from the last $w$ days; read VaR and
ES straight off that sample, optionally down-weighting old days.}

\paragraph{Plain HS.} With $r_{(1)}\le\cdots\le r_{(w)}$ the order statistics of
$W_t$,
\begin{equation}
  \VaR_{\alpha,t} = r_{(\lceil \alpha w\rceil)},
  \qquad
  \ES_{\alpha,t} = \frac{1}{\lceil\alpha w\rceil}\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil\alpha w\rceil} r_{(j)} .
\end{equation}
No distributional assumption; the only choice is $w$, which trades tail
resolution (large $w$: more tail observations, less responsive) against
adaptivity (small $w$: reacts to regime change, noisier tail).

\paragraph{Age-weighted HS} (Boudoukh--Richardson--Whitelaw, 1998). Observation
$s$ at age $a_s = t-1-s$ gets weight $w_s \propto \lambda^{a_s}$ with
$\lambda = 2^{-1/H}$ ($H$ the half-life, $125$ days), normalised to
$\sum_s w_s = 1$. The weighted quantile is the smallest $r_{(k)}$ with
$\sum_{j\le k} w_{(j)} \ge \alpha$, and the ES is the corresponding weighted tail
mean. The effective sample size $\big(\sum w_s\big)^2/\sum w_s^2 \approx H/\ln 2$
is much smaller than $w$, so AWHS reacts faster at the cost of a noisier tail.

\intuition{``What has actually happened lately, ranked by severity.'' The window
length, and the decay, are the only knobs.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{EWMA / RiskMetrics}

\inbrief{Track variance as an exponentially fading average of squared returns,
then put a normal (or fixed-$\nu$ $t$) tail on it.}

Zero conditional mean, one-parameter variance:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{t+1}^2 = \lambda\,\sigma_t^2 + (1-\lambda)\,r_t^2,
  \qquad \lambda = 0.94 .
\end{equation}
This is GARCH(1,1) with $\omega=0$, $\alpha = 1-\lambda$, $\beta = \lambda$, so
$\alpha+\beta=1$: an \emph{integrated} GARCH, with no mean reversion and no
finite unconditional variance. The predictive law is
$r_{t+1}\sim\sigma_{t+1}Z$, $Z\sim N(0,1)$ (or a fixed-$\nu$ standardized $t$),
so
\begin{equation}
  \VaR_{\alpha,t} = \sigma_{t+1}\,\Phi^{-1}(\alpha),
  \qquad
  \ES_{\alpha,t} = -\sigma_{t+1}\,\frac{\phi(\Phi^{-1}(\alpha))}{\alpha}.
\end{equation}

\intuition{An exponentially fading average of squared returns. A strong level
forecaster and the industry baseline, but the Gaussian tail is too thin for
$\alpha=0.01$.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Single-regime GARCH with standardized \texorpdfstring{$t$}{t} innovations}\label{sec:garch}

\inbrief{Let today's variance depend on yesterday's shock and yesterday's
variance (with a bad-news bonus in GJR/EGARCH), and draw the return from a
fat-tailed $t$ whose thickness is estimated.}

\paragraph{Specification.} $r_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t$,
$\varepsilon_t = \sqrt{h_t}\,\eta_t$, $\eta_t\stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim}$
standardized $t_\nu$ (defined below). The three variants:
\begin{align}
  \text{GARCH-}t:\ & h_t = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,h_{t-1},
                     &&\text{stationary if } \alpha+\beta<1,\ \omega>0, \\
  \text{GJR-}t:\ & h_t = \omega + (\alpha + \gamma\,\Ind{\varepsilon_{t-1}<0})\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,h_{t-1},
                     &&\text{stationary if } \alpha + \tfrac12\gamma + \beta<1, \\
  \text{EGARCH-}t:\ & \ln h_t = \omega + \alpha\big(|\eta_{t-1}|-\E|\eta_{t-1}|\big) + \gamma\,\eta_{t-1} + \beta\,\ln h_{t-1},
                     &&\text{stationary if } |\beta|<1 .
\end{align}
GJR's $\gamma>0$ is the leverage term (bad news raises variance more). EGARCH
models $\ln h_t$, so $h_t>0$ needs no parameter constraints, and $\gamma$
captures a sign asymmetry directly.

\paragraph{The standardized $t$.} If $T\sim t_\nu$ (raw, $\Var T = \nu/(\nu-2)$),
then $\eta = T\sqrt{(\nu-2)/\nu}$ has unit variance and density
\begin{equation}
  g_\nu(x) = \frac{\Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{\nu+1}{2}\big)}
                  {\Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{\nu}{2}\big)\sqrt{\pi(\nu-2)}}
             \left(1 + \frac{x^2}{\nu-2}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}},
             \qquad \nu > 2 .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Estimation.} Quasi-ML on the window (returns scaled by $100$ for the
optimiser): maximise
\begin{equation}
  \ell(\theta) = \sum_{s\in W_t}\left[
     \ln g_\nu\!\left(\frac{r_s - \mu}{\sqrt{h_s(\theta)}}\right)
     - \tfrac12\ln h_s(\theta)\right],
\end{equation}
with $h_s(\theta)$ from the recursion above (seeded at the sample variance).

\paragraph{Forecast.} With $q_\alpha = t_\nu^{-1}(\alpha)$ the raw-$t$ quantile
and $f_\nu$ its density,
\begin{equation}
  z_\alpha = \sqrt{\tfrac{\nu-2}{\nu}}\;q_\alpha,
  \qquad
  \bar z_\alpha
    = -\sqrt{\tfrac{\nu-2}{\nu}}\;\frac{f_\nu(q_\alpha)}{\alpha}\,
      \frac{\nu + q_\alpha^2}{\nu-1},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \VaR_{\alpha,t} = \mu + \sqrt{h_{t+1}}\;z_\alpha,
  \qquad
  \ES_{\alpha,t} = \mu + \sqrt{h_{t+1}}\;\bar z_\alpha .
\end{equation}
\emph{Omitting the $\sqrt{(\nu-2)/\nu}$ factor} --- the v1 error --- multiplies
both by $\sqrt{\nu/(\nu-2)}$, i.e.\ $\approx1.73$ at $\nu=3$: the VaR becomes far
too wide and the breach rate collapses. Guards: if the fit fails, if
$\nu\le2.05$, or if $\sqrt{h_{t+1}}$ exceeds $20\times$ the window s.d.\
(EGARCH's log-variance can diverge near non-stationarity), fall back to the
empirical quantile.

\intuition{GARCH: today's variance is a weighted memory of yesterday's shock and
yesterday's variance. GJR/EGARCH: a bad shock counts more. The $t$: replace the
Gaussian tail with a fat one whose thickness $\nu$ is estimated.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Filtered Historical Simulation}

\inbrief{Use GARCH only to remove the volatility dynamics, then read the tail
off the leftover shocks --- no assumed shape for them.}

Barone-Adesi--Giannopoulos--Vosper (1999). Fit a GARCH(1,1) filter, form the
standardized residuals $\hat z_s = \hat\varepsilon_s/\sqrt{\hat h_s}$,
$s\in W_t$, and rescale their empirical tail by the one-step volatility:
\begin{equation}
  \VaR_{\alpha,t} = \hat\mu + \sqrt{\hat h_{t+1}}\;\widehat Q_\alpha(\{\hat z_s\}),
  \qquad
  \ES_{\alpha,t} = \hat\mu + \sqrt{\hat h_{t+1}}\;
     \frac{\sum_s \hat z_s\Ind{\hat z_s\le\widehat Q_\alpha}}
          {\sum_s \Ind{\hat z_s\le\widehat Q_\alpha}} .
\end{equation}
The residual distribution is left non-parametric; only the volatility dynamics
are parametric. This is the standard trading-desk method.

\intuition{Let GARCH strip the volatility dynamics, then read the tail off the
leftover shocks without assuming a shape for them.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{GARCH-EVT (McNeil--Frey, 2000)}\label{sec:evt}

\inbrief{Same GARCH filter as FHS, but fit the \emph{only} distribution
extreme-value theory allows for ``exceedances over a high threshold'' (the
Generalized Pareto) to the worst residuals, and invert it for VaR/ES.}

\paragraph{Peaks-over-threshold.} Work in loss units $L = -\hat z$. For a
threshold $u$, the excess distribution is
$F_u(y) = \Prob(L - u \le y \mid L > u)$.

\begin{theorem}[Pickands, 1975; Balkema \& de Haan, 1974]
For a broad class of $F$ (all common continuous distributions with a regularly
varying or exponential-type tail), there is a positive function $\beta(u)$ with
\begin{equation}
  \sup_{0\le y < x_F - u}\big| F_u(y) - G_{\xi,\beta(u)}(y)\big| \;\longrightarrow\; 0
  \qquad (u\uparrow x_F),
\end{equation}
where $G_{\xi,\beta}$ is the Generalized Pareto Distribution,
\begin{equation}
  G_{\xi,\beta}(y) =
  \begin{cases}
    1 - \big(1 + \xi y/\beta\big)^{-1/\xi}, & \xi\neq0,\\[2pt]
    1 - e^{-y/\beta}, & \xi = 0,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad y\ge0\ (\text{and } y < -\beta/\xi \text{ if } \xi<0).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
$\xi>0$ is a heavy (Fréchet-type) tail, $\xi=0$ light (Gumbel), $\xi<0$ bounded.

\paragraph{Tail and risk estimators.} With $N_u$ of the $n$ residuals above $u$,
and $(\hat\xi,\hat\beta)$ fitted to the exceedances by ML, the tail probability
for $x>u$ is estimated by
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\Prob}(L>x)
  = \frac{N_u}{n}\Big(1 + \hat\xi\,\frac{x-u}{\hat\beta}\Big)^{-1/\hat\xi}.
\end{equation}
Setting this to $\alpha$ and solving for $x$ gives, for $\alpha < N_u/n$,
\begin{equation}
  \VaR^{L}_\alpha
  = u + \frac{\hat\beta}{\hat\xi}
      \left[\Big(\frac{\alpha}{N_u/n}\Big)^{-\hat\xi} - 1\right],
  \qquad
  \ES^{L}_\alpha
  = \frac{\VaR^{L}_\alpha}{1-\hat\xi} + \frac{\hat\beta - \hat\xi\,u}{1-\hat\xi}
  \quad (\hat\xi<1),
\end{equation}
with the $\hat\xi\to0$ limit
$\VaR^{L}_\alpha = u + \hat\beta\ln\!\big(\tfrac{N_u/n}{\alpha}\big)$. Translate
back: the standardized-residual quantile is $-\VaR^{L}_\alpha$, and
\begin{equation}
  \VaR_{\alpha,t} = \hat\mu + \sqrt{\hat h_{t+1}}\,\big({-}\VaR^{L}_\alpha\big),
  \qquad
  \ES_{\alpha,t} = \hat\mu + \sqrt{\hat h_{t+1}}\,\big({-}\ES^{L}_\alpha\big).
\end{equation}
We fix $u$ at the $90\%$ empirical loss quantile. Lower $u$: more exceedances,
lower variance but more bias (the GPD limit is worse). Higher $u$: cleaner limit,
few points. When $\alpha\ge N_u/n$ (the quantile is in the body) or the fit
degenerates ($\hat\beta\le0$, non-finite $\hat\xi$), the model falls back to the
empirical residual quantile. For the PIT, the CDF is piecewise: the empirical
residual CDF for $L\le u$, the GPD expression above for $L>u$.

\intuition{Extreme-value theory gives the \emph{only} asymptotically valid shape
for ``exceedances over a high threshold.'' We fit that shape to the
GARCH-filtered shocks, so the tail is estimated from the tail rather than
extrapolated from the centre.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Merton jump-diffusion}

\inbrief{Return $=$ a normal ``diffusion'' part $+$ a Poisson number of normal
``jumps''; the density is a Poisson-weighted mixture of normals, fit by maximum
likelihood.}

\paragraph{Model.} Under $\Prob$,
\begin{equation}
  r_{t+1} = \Big(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2 - \lambda\bar k\Big)
          + \sigma\,\varepsilon_{t+1}
          + \sum_{j=1}^{N_{t+1}} J_j,
  \qquad
  N_{t+1}\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda),\quad J_j\stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim}N(\mu_J,\sigma_J^2),
\end{equation}
with the compensator $\bar k = e^{\mu_J + \sigma_J^2/2} - 1$ so that the jump
part has zero mean contribution to the drift. Conditioning on $N_{t+1}=n$ makes
$r_{t+1}$ Gaussian, so its density is a Poisson mixture of normals:
\begin{equation}
  f(r) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^n}{n!}\;
         \varphi\!\big(r;\ \mu - \lambda\bar k + n\mu_J,\ \sigma^2 + n\sigma_J^2\big).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Estimation.} Truncate the sum at $n = 10$ and maximise
$\sum_{s\in W_t}\ln f(r_s)$ over $(\lambda,\mu_J,\sigma_J)$ by L-BFGS-B
($\mu,\sigma$ set to the window mean and s.d.). The density is evaluated as a
matrix product over $n$, not a Python loop, which is a $\sim10^3\times$ speed-up
over a naive implementation.

\paragraph{Forecast.} $F_t$ is represented by $2\times10^4$ Monte-Carlo draws of
$r_{t+1}$; $\VaR$, $\ES$, and the CDF are read off the sample.
$\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \sigma^2 + \lambda(\mu_J^2 + \sigma_J^2)$ in closed form.

\intuition{Some crypto down-days are discrete events, not big draws from a
continuous law. The Poisson term models them, on a shorter time scale than
GARCH clustering.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{HAR-RV and HARQ}\label{sec:har}

\inbrief{Predict tomorrow's realized variance by an OLS regression on its own
daily, weekly and monthly averages; HARQ shrinks the daily term on days when
$\RV$ was itself measured noisily.}

\paragraph{HAR-RV} (Corsi, 2009). The heterogeneous-market hypothesis: agents
act on daily, weekly and monthly horizons, and volatility cascades across them.
The model regresses $\RV$ on three moving averages:
\begin{equation}
  \RV_t = \beta_0
        + \beta_d\,\RV_{t-1}
        + \beta_w\,\overline{\RV}^{(w)}_{t-1}
        + \beta_m\,\overline{\RV}^{(m)}_{t-1}
        + \varepsilon_t,
\end{equation}
with $\overline{\RV}^{(w)}_{t-1}=\tfrac15\sum_{i=1}^{5}\RV_{t-i}$,
$\overline{\RV}^{(m)}_{t-1}=\tfrac1{22}\sum_{i=1}^{22}\RV_{t-i}$, fitted by OLS
on the window (in $\RV$ levels). Three lags approximate long memory without a
fractional model.

\paragraph{HARQ} (Bollerslev--Patton--Quaedvlieg, 2016). The OLS estimate of
$\beta_d$ is attenuated by the measurement error in $\RV_{t-1}$
($\RV = \IV + \text{noise}$, noise variance $\propto \RQ$). HARQ makes the daily
coefficient shrink on noisy days:
\begin{equation}
  \RV_t = \beta_0
        + \big(\beta_d + \beta_{dQ}\,\widetilde{\RQ}^{\,1/2}_{t-1}\big)\RV_{t-1}
        + \beta_w\,\overline{\RV}^{(w)}_{t-1}
        + \beta_m\,\overline{\RV}^{(m)}_{t-1}
        + \varepsilon_t,
\end{equation}
where $\widetilde{\RQ}^{\,1/2}$ is $\RQ^{1/2}$ standardised to zero mean and unit
variance so the interaction is on the same scale as the other regressors.

\paragraph{Forecast.} $\widehat{\RV}_{t+1}$ is the one-step OLS prediction; it is
$\sigma_{t+1}^2$. It is floored at half the $5\%$ RV quantile and capped at
$30\times$ the median RV. The return law is $\sqrt{\sigma_{t+1}^2}$ times a
standardized $t_\nu$, with $\nu$ from a $t$-fit to $\{r_s/\sqrt{\RV_s}\}$ on the
window.

\intuition{Volatility is persistent at several horizons at once; a linear model
in the daily, weekly and monthly averages captures most of it. HARQ adds: on a
day when the variance estimate itself was noisy, lean on it less.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Realized GARCH (Hansen--Huang--Shek, 2012)}

\inbrief{A GARCH that updates its variance from observed $\RV$ instead of the
squared return, plus a ``measurement equation'' that keeps model-variance and
$\RV$ mutually consistent; both equations fit jointly by maximum likelihood.}

A GARCH whose latent variance is tied to observed $\RV$ by a \emph{measurement
equation}. With $z_t = r_t/\sqrt{h_t}$, $z_t\sim N(0,1)$, $x_t = \RV_t$:
\begin{align}
  r_t &= \sqrt{h_t}\,z_t, \\
  \ln h_t &= \omega + \beta\,\ln h_{t-1} + \gamma\,\ln x_{t-1}, \\
  \ln x_t &= \xi + \varphi\,\ln h_t + \tau_1 z_t + \tau_2(z_t^2 - 1) + u_t,
            \qquad u_t\sim N(0,\sigma_u^2),
\end{align}
where $\tau(z)=\tau_1 z + \tau_2(z^2-1)$ is a leverage function
($\E[\tau(z)]=0$). The eight parameters
$(\omega,\beta,\gamma,\xi,\varphi,\tau_1,\tau_2,\sigma_u^2)$ are estimated by
joint Gaussian ML,
\begin{equation}
  \ell = -\tfrac12\sum_t\big(\ln h_t + z_t^2\big)
         -\tfrac12\sum_t\Big(\ln(2\pi\sigma_u^2) + u_t^2/\sigma_u^2\Big),
\end{equation}
subject to $\beta+\gamma<1$ (persistence). The $\ln h_t$ recursion is AR(1) with
a time-varying input $\omega + \gamma\ln x_{t-1}$, so it is evaluated as a linear
filter. Forecast:
$h_{t+1}=\exp(\omega + \beta\ln h_t + \gamma\ln x_t)$; the return law is
$\sqrt{h_{t+1}}$ times a $t_\nu$ fitted to $\{z_t\}$.

\intuition{Ordinary GARCH updates variance from the squared return --- a very
noisy signal. Realized GARCH updates it from $\RV$, and the measurement equation
keeps the two notions of variance consistent.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{GARCH-X}

\inbrief{Plain GARCH-$t$ with one extra term --- lagged $\RV$ --- in the
variance equation; its coefficient $\gamma$ measures whether measured variance
adds anything beyond the squared return.}

GARCH(1,1)-$t$ with a lagged realized variance in the variance equation:
\begin{equation}
  h_t = \omega + \alpha\,r_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,h_{t-1} + \gamma\,x_{t-1},
  \qquad x_t = \RV_t,\quad r_t/\sqrt{h_t}\sim\text{standardized }t_\nu,
\end{equation}
with $\omega>0$, $\alpha,\beta,\gamma\ge0$, $\alpha+\beta<1$. Estimated by ML
(hand-coded --- \texttt{arch} has no variance-equation exogenous term); the
recursion is a linear filter. Without a realized block, $\gamma$ is fixed at $0$
and the model \emph{is} GARCH-$t$. Forecast and guards as in \S\ref{sec:garch}.

\intuition{The minimal test of ``does yesterday's \emph{measured} variance add
anything beyond yesterday's squared return?'' --- $\gamma$ answers it.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{CAViaR and CAViaR-X (Engle--Manganelli, 2004)}

\inbrief{Skip the density entirely: write the $\alpha$-quantile itself as an
autoregression and fit it by minimising the pinball loss so the empirical breach
rate lands on $\alpha$.}

\paragraph{Model.} The conditional quantile itself follows an autoregression.
Two specifications, with $(x)^{+}=\max(x,0)$, $(x)^{-}=\max(-x,0)$:
\begin{align}
  \text{SAV:}\quad & q_t = \beta_1 + \beta_2\,q_{t-1} + \beta_3\,|r_{t-1}|,\\
  \text{AS:}\quad  & q_t = \beta_1 + \beta_2\,q_{t-1} + \beta_3\,(r_{t-1})^{+} + \beta_4\,(r_{t-1})^{-},
\end{align}
and CAViaR-X appends $\beta_5\,x_{t-1}$ with $x_{t-1}=\sqrt{\RV_{t-1}}$.

\paragraph{Estimation.} The population $\alpha$-quantile minimises the
\emph{tick} (pinball / check) loss $\rho_\alpha(u)=u(\alpha-\Ind{u<0})$. So the
parameters solve
\begin{equation}
  \hat\theta = \argmin_\theta \ \frac{1}{|W_t|}\sum_{s\in W_t}
     \big(\alpha - \Ind{r_s < q_s(\theta)}\big)\big(r_s - q_s(\theta)\big),
\end{equation}
by Nelder--Mead from two starts. Under regularity (Engle \& Manganelli, 2004),
$\hat\theta$ is consistent and asymptotically normal. The recursion
$q_t = \beta_1 + \beta_2 q_{t-1} + (\text{news})_{t-1}$ is AR(1) with a
time-varying input and is evaluated with a linear filter, which is what makes
the search tractable. CAViaR is fit once \emph{per} $\alpha$; a monotonicity
repair enforces $q^{(\alpha')}\le q^{(\alpha)}$ for $\alpha'<\alpha$ (independent
quantile regressions can cross).

\paragraph{Expected Shortfall.} CAViaR has no ES by construction; we scale the
one-step quantile by the in-sample tail-to-quantile ratio,
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\ES}_{\alpha,t} = q_{t+1}\cdot\hat\rho,
  \qquad
  \hat\rho = \frac{\overline{\,r_s \mid r_s < q_s\,}}{\overline{\,q_s \mid r_s < q_s\,}}
             \in [1,3]\ (\text{clipped}),
\end{equation}
a simple analogue of the Righi--Ceretta (2013) idea.

\intuition{Skip the density: write down how the $2.5\%$ worst outcome evolves
--- it is persistent and it widens after big moves --- and fit that curve so the
empirical breach rate comes out at $2.5\%$.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{MS-GARCH (2-state Markov-switching)}\label{sec:msgarch}

\inbrief{Two GARCH regimes (calm / turbulent) with a hidden Markov switch
between them; a filter keeps a running probability of each regime and the
forecast is the probability-weighted mixture of the two densities.}

\paragraph{Model.} A latent state $S_t\in\{1,2\}$ follows a first-order Markov
chain with transition matrix $P=(p_{k\ell})$, $p_{k\ell}=\Prob(S_t=\ell\mid
S_{t-1}=k)$. Conditional on the state the variance is a separate GARCH(1,1) with
$t$ innovations. To avoid the path-dependence of the naive specification (each
regime's $h$ would depend on the entire unobserved state history), we use the
Haas--Mittnik--Paolella (2004) form, in which each regime's recursion is driven
by the \emph{observed} return:
\begin{equation}
  r_t \mid (\Fcal_{t-1},S_t=k) \sim \sqrt{h_{k,t}}\;\eta_{k,t},
  \qquad
  h_{k,t} = \omega_k + \alpha_k\,r_{t-1}^2 + \beta_k\,h_{k,t-1},
  \qquad
  \eta_{k,t}\sim g_{\nu_k}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Hamilton filter.} Let $\pi_{k,t\mid t}=\Prob(S_t=k\mid\Fcal_t)$ and
$f_k(r_t)\eqdef h_{k,t}^{-1/2} g_{\nu_k}(r_t/\sqrt{h_{k,t}})$. Iterate:
\begin{align}
  \text{predict:}\quad &
    \pi_{k,t\mid t-1} = \sum_{\ell} p_{\ell k}\,\pi_{\ell,t-1\mid t-1}, \\
  \text{update:}\quad &
    \pi_{k,t\mid t} = \frac{\pi_{k,t\mid t-1}\,f_k(r_t)}
                           {\sum_j \pi_{j,t\mid t-1}\,f_j(r_t)}, \\
  \text{log-likelihood term:}\quad &
    \ell_t = \ln\!\Big(\sum_j \pi_{j,t\mid t-1}\,f_j(r_t)\Big).
\end{align}
The parameters $(\omega_k,\alpha_k,\beta_k,\nu_k)_{k=1,2}$ and $P$ are estimated
by maximising $\sum_t \ell_t$ (in R, package \texttt{MSGARCH}).

\paragraph{Forecast.} The one-step predictive density is the mixture
\begin{equation}
  f_t(r) = \sum_{k=1}^{2}\pi_{k,t+1\mid t}\;
           h_{k,t+1}^{-1/2}\,g_{\nu_k}\!\big(r/\sqrt{h_{k,t+1}}\big),
  \qquad
  \pi_{\cdot,t+1\mid t} = P^{\top}\pi_{\cdot,t\mid t},
\end{equation}
and $\VaR_{\alpha,t}$, $\ES_{\alpha,t}$ are obtained numerically from its CDF.
The walk-forward re-fits every $20$ days and re-filters daily on appended
observations; regimes are labelled by stationary variance
$\omega_k/(1-\alpha_k-\beta_k)$ each re-fit (label switching). In the Python
pipeline a bridge model serves the cached R predictions; dates without one fall
back to the empirical quantile.

\paragraph{The regime probability.} The filtered probability
$\Prob(S_t=k^\star\mid\Fcal_t)$ of the high-variance regime is recorded. From a
single full-sample fit it correlates $\approx0.7$ with $|r_t|$; from the
rolling-window walk-forward it does not ($\approx 0.07$) --- see
\S\ref{sec:limits}. The descriptive regime curve therefore uses the full-sample
fit and is kept out of the VaR backtest.

\intuition{Markets alternate between a calm regime and a turbulent one, and
which one you are in is not observed. The model carries a probability over both
and mixes their forecasts.}

\subsection{Unconditional coverage --- Kupiec POF (1995)}\label{sec:kupiec}

$H_0:\Prob(I_t=1)=\alpha$. Since $x\mid H_0\sim\text{Binomial}(n,\alpha)$, the
likelihood ratio is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{LR}_{uc}
  = -2\ln\frac{(1-\alpha)^{n-x}\alpha^{x}}
              {(1-\hat\pi)^{n-x}\hat\pi^{x}}
  \;\dto\; \chi^2_1 .
\end{equation}
Power comes from a mismatch between $\hat\pi$ and $\alpha$; the test is
uninformative when $x\in\{0,n\}$ (then $\mathrm{LR}_{uc}$ is set to NaN).

\intuition{Did the $2.5\%$ line get crossed about $2.5\%$ of the time? Says
nothing about \emph{when}.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Independence and conditional coverage --- Christoffersen (1998)}

Let $n_{ij}$ count $I_{t-1}=i\to I_t=j$, $\hat\pi_{ij}=n_{ij}/(n_{i0}+n_{i1})$,
$\hat\pi=(n_{01}+n_{11})/n$. Against a first-order Markov alternative,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{LR}_{ind}
  = -2\ln\frac{(1-\hat\pi)^{n_{00}+n_{10}}\hat\pi^{n_{01}+n_{11}}}
              {(1-\hat\pi_{01})^{n_{00}}\hat\pi_{01}^{n_{01}}
               (1-\hat\pi_{11})^{n_{10}}\hat\pi_{11}^{n_{11}}}
  \;\dto\;\chi^2_1,
\end{equation}
and conditional coverage combines them:
$\mathrm{LR}_{cc}=\mathrm{LR}_{uc}+\mathrm{LR}_{ind}\dto\chi^2_2$. Limitation:
only $1$-lag dependence; longer clustering needs the DQ test.

\intuition{If a violation today makes one tomorrow more likely, the model is
missing volatility clustering even when the average rate is fine.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Dynamic Quantile test --- Engle--Manganelli (2004)}

The sharpest of the coverage tests. Under \eqref{eq:mds} the demeaned hit
$\mathrm{Hit}_t = I_t - \alpha$ is a martingale difference, so it is orthogonal
to everything in $\Fcal_{t-1}$. Regress it on a constant, its $p$ lags, and the
contemporaneous VaR:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Hit}_t = \delta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{p}\delta_j\,\mathrm{Hit}_{t-j}
                 + \delta_{p+1}\,\VaR_{\alpha,t} + \epsilon_t .
\end{equation}
Under $H_0$ all $\delta = 0$ and $\Var(\mathrm{Hit}_t)=\alpha(1-\alpha)$, so with
$X$ the regressor matrix,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{DQ} = \frac{\hat\delta^{\top}(X^{\top}X)\,\hat\delta}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}
  \;\dto\;\chi^2_{p+2}
  \qquad (p = 4).
\end{equation}

\intuition{Kupiec and Christoffersen only look at the hit series; DQ also asks
whether the hits can be \emph{predicted} --- from recent hits, or from the VaR
level. A model that breaches mostly when its own VaR happens to be shallow fails
DQ.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Basel traffic light}

Exceptions in a rolling $250$-day window: $\le 4$ green, $5$--$9$ amber, $\ge 10$
red, mapping to a capital-multiplier add-on $0$ / $0.40$--$0.85$ / $1.0$. A
regulatory rule, used in the capital layer (\S\ref{sec:decision}).

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Expected-Shortfall backtests --- Acerbi--Székely (2014)}

ES alone is not elicitable, so these are diagnostics, not scores. With
$v_t=\VaR_{\alpha,t}<0$, $e_t=\ES_{\alpha,t}<0$, breach set
$\mathcal B=\{t:r_t<v_t\}$,
\begin{equation}
  Z_2 = -\frac{1}{n\alpha}\sum_{t\in\mathcal B}\frac{r_t}{e_t} + 1,
  \qquad
  Z_1 = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal B|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal B}\frac{r_t}{e_t} + 1 .
\end{equation}
On a breach $r_t\approx e_t$, so $r_t/e_t\approx1$, and with $\approx n\alpha$
breaches $Z_2\approx-1+1=0$; likewise $Z_1$. Formally $\E[Z_1]=\E[Z_2]=0$ under
a correct ES; $Z_2$ folds in the breach frequency, $Z_1$ conditions on being in
the tail. $Z<0\Rightarrow$ realized tail losses worse than forecast. The null
has no clean closed form; the one-sided $p$-value is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\Ind{Z^{(b)}\le Z^{\text{obs}}},
\end{equation}
over $B$ return paths simulated from the model's own predictive distribution at
each date (the study layer builds these).

\intuition{The model says ``a breach costs about $e_t$.'' Compare with how much
was actually lost on breach days. If the average realized breach is $30\%$
deeper than $e_t$, $Z_2$ is clearly negative.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Consistent scoring: the FZ0 loss}\label{sec:fz}

\inbrief{A single per-day loss for the $(\VaR,\ES)$ pair that is provably
minimised only by the true values, so its average over the out-of-sample period
is a legitimate score to rank models by.}

\paragraph{Joint elicitability.} $\VaR_\alpha$ is elicitable (pinball loss);
$\ES_\alpha$ is not (Gneiting, 2011). But:
\begin{theorem}[Fissler \& Ziegel, 2016]
The pair $(\VaR_\alpha,\ES_\alpha)$ is jointly elicitable. Every strictly
consistent scoring function has the form
\begin{equation}
  S(v,e;r) = \big(\Ind{r\le v} - \alpha\big)\big(G_1(v) - G_1(r)\big)
           + G_2(e)\Big(e - v + \tfrac1\alpha\Ind{r\le v}(v - r)\Big)
           - \mathcal G_2(e) + a(r),
\end{equation}
for $G_1$ increasing, $G_2 = \mathcal G_2'$ increasing and convex, and $a$
arbitrary (integrable).
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{FZ0.} Take $G_1\equiv0$ and $G_2(x) = -1/x$ (so
$\mathcal G_2(x) = -\ln(-x)$), drop $a(r)$, and simplify. With $v,e<0$,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  L^{\mathrm{FZ0}}_t
   = \frac{\Ind{r_t\le v_t}}{\alpha\,e_t}\,(r_t - v_t)
   + \frac{v_t}{e_t} + \ln(-e_t) - 1 . \;}
\end{equation}
The breach term is positive ($1/(\alpha e_t)<0$ and $r_t - v_t<0$ on a breach),
so a too-shallow VaR with many breaches is penalised. FZ0 is $0$-homogeneous
(invariant to a common positive rescaling of $(r,v,e)$), which makes average
losses comparable across assets and volatility regimes. Lower average
$L^{\mathrm{FZ0}}$ is unambiguously better.

\paragraph{QLIKE for variance.} For ranking \emph{variance} forecasts against a
realized proxy, use
\begin{equation}
  L^{\mathrm{QL}}_t = \frac{\RV_t}{\sigma_t^2} - \ln\frac{\RV_t}{\sigma_t^2} - 1 \;\ge\;0,
\end{equation}
which, with MSE, is one of the few losses in the Bregman family that give the
correct ranking when the proxy $\RV$ is a conditionally unbiased but noisy
estimate of the true variance (Patton, 2011). QLIKE penalises under-prediction
more heavily.

\intuition{A consistent loss cannot be gamed: the way to make it small \emph{is}
to report the true VaR and ES. Averaging it over the out-of-sample period turns
``which model is better'' into a number.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Diebold--Mariano (1995)}

For two models with loss difference $d_t = L^{A}_t - L^{B}_t$, test
$H_0:\E[d_t]=0$. The long-run variance of $\bar d$ is estimated with a
Newey--West (Bartlett-kernel) HAC:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\Var}(\bar d)
  = \frac1n\Big(\hat\gamma_0 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{L}\big(1 - \tfrac{k}{L+1}\big)\hat\gamma_k\Big),
  \qquad
  \hat\gamma_k = \frac1n\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}(d_t - \bar d)(d_{t-k} - \bar d),
\end{equation}
with $L = \lceil n^{1/3}\rceil$. The statistic $\bar d/\sqrt{\widehat{\Var}(\bar
d)}$ is multiplied by the Harvey--Leybourne--Newbold (1997) small-sample factor
\begin{equation}
  \sqrt{\frac{n + 1 - 2L + L(L-1)/n}{n}}
\end{equation}
and referred to $t_{n-1}$; we report the two-sided $p$-value.

\intuition{One model's average loss being lower is not enough --- the difference
must be large relative to its variability over time, and loss differences are
autocorrelated, hence the HAC variance.}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Model Confidence Set --- Hansen--Lunde--Nason (2011)}\label{sec:mcs}

\inbrief{Instead of crowning one winner, return the group of models that are
statistically tied for best: repeatedly test ``are the survivors equally
accurate?'', drop the worst while the answer is no, stop when it is yes.}

\paragraph{Goal.} Rather than name a single winner, isolate the set
$\widehat{\Mcal}_{1-\beta}$ of models that cannot be statistically separated
from the best. It has the property
$\liminf \Prob\big(\Mcal^{\ast}\subseteq\widehat{\Mcal}_{1-\beta}\big)\ge 1-\beta$,
where $\Mcal^{\ast}$ is the true set of models with minimal expected loss.

\paragraph{Objects.} For the current set $\Mcal$ with per-day losses
$L_{i,t}$, define the relative loss and its time average
\begin{equation}
  d_{i,t} = L_{i,t} - \frac{1}{|\Mcal|}\sum_{j\in\Mcal} L_{j,t},
  \qquad
  \bar d_i = \frac1T\sum_t d_{i,t},
  \qquad
  t_i = \frac{\bar d_i}{\sqrt{\widehat{\operatorname{var}}^{\ast}(\bar d_i)}},
\end{equation}
with $\widehat{\operatorname{var}}^{\ast}$ the bootstrap variance below. Two
equivalence statistics are in the literature; we use the range-of-$t$ form
\begin{equation}
  T_{\max,\Mcal} = \max_{i\in\Mcal} t_i
  \qquad\Big(\text{the alternative is } T_{R,\Mcal}=\max_{i,j\in\Mcal}\big|\bar d_{ij}\big|/\sqrt{\widehat{\operatorname{var}}(\bar d_{ij})}\Big).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Stationary block bootstrap} (Politis \& Romano, 1994). To resample
the time dimension while preserving dependence: draw block lengths
$L_b\sim\text{Geometric}(1/\ell)$ (mean block length $\ell = 20$) and starting
points uniformly on $\{1,\dots,T\}$; concatenate blocks, wrapping around
$\bmod\,T$, until length $T$. Do this $B = 5{,}000$ times; each replicate
$b$ gives bootstrap means $\bar d_i^{\ast(b)}$, and
$\widehat{\operatorname{var}}^{\ast}(\bar d_i)$ is their sample variance.

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item Compute $T_{\max}$ and its bootstrap $p$-value
      $p = \tfrac1B\sum_b \Ind{T_{\max}^{\ast(b)} \ge T_{\max}}$, where
      $T_{\max}^{\ast(b)} = \max_i (\bar d_i^{\ast(b)} - \bar d_i)/
      \sqrt{\widehat{\operatorname{var}}^{\ast}(\bar d_i)}$.
\item Set $p_{\text{run}}\leftarrow\max(p_{\text{run}}, p)$. If
      $p_{\text{run}}\ge\beta$, stop: every model in $\Mcal$ is in
      $\widehat{\Mcal}_{1-\beta}$.
\item Otherwise eliminate $\argmax_i t_i$ (the model with the largest excess
      loss), record its \emph{MCS $p$-value} as $p_{\text{run}}$, and repeat.
\end{enumerate}
The running maximum makes the reported MCS $p$-values monotone in elimination
order; a model is in the set at level $1-\beta$ iff its MCS $p$-value
$\ge\beta$. This is run on the FZ0 losses (the $(\VaR,\ES)$ ranking) and,
separately, on the QLIKE losses (the variance ranking), per asset and per
$\alpha$.

\intuition{A tournament with confidence intervals. Repeatedly ask ``do the
models still in contention forecast equally well?''; while the answer is no,
drop the worst; when it becomes yes, whoever is left is the confidence set. Much
data $\Rightarrow$ a small set of genuine leaders; little data $\Rightarrow$ a
large, uninformative set.}

\subsection{Density calibration --- Berkowitz (2001)}\label{sec:pit}

If the whole predictive density is right, $u_t = F_{t-1}(r_t)$ is i.i.d.\
$\mathrm{U}(0,1)$, so $z_t = \Phi^{-1}(u_t)$ is i.i.d.\ $N(0,1)$. Fit an AR(1)
$z_t = \mu + \rho z_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$, $\epsilon_t\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$, by ML and
LR-test $H_0:(\mu,\rho,\sigma^2)=(0,0,1)$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{LR}_{B} = -2\big(\ell(0,0,1) - \ell(\hat\mu,\hat\rho,\hat\sigma^2)\big)
  \;\dto\;\chi^2_3 ,
\end{equation}
with $\ell(0,0,1) = -\tfrac12\big(n\ln 2\pi + \sum_t z_t^2\big)$ and
$\ell(\hat\mu,\hat\rho,\hat\sigma^2) = -\tfrac12\big(n\ln 2\pi + n\ln\hat\sigma^2 + n\big)$.
Rejection points to drift ($\mu\neq0$), memory ($\rho\neq0$), or wrong spread
($\sigma^2\neq1$) in the PIT.

\intuition{One test for the whole forecast distribution, not just the tail:
after mapping outcomes through the model's own CDF, what remains should be
independent standard-normal noise.}

\section{From forecasts to decisions}\label{sec:decision}

The study's output feeds a light decision layer (specified here, built in a
later phase).

\paragraph{Capital.} FRTB Internal-Models style: for notional $N$ and a
liquidity horizon $\text{LH}\ge10$ business days,
\begin{equation}
  \text{Capital} = m_c \cdot N \cdot \big|\ES_{97.5\%,\ \text{LH}}\big|,
  \qquad
  m_c = 1.5 + \text{(Basel traffic-light add-on)},
\end{equation}
with $\ES_{\text{LH}}$ reported both by $\sqrt{\text{LH}/10}$ scaling and by
path simulation. A \emph{model-risk add-on} equal to the capital spread between
the best and worst model in the MCS plays the role of a prudent-valuation
adjustment.

\paragraph{Limits.} The maximum notional $N^{\ast}$ solves
$N^{\ast}\,|\ES_{99\%,1\text{d}}| = \text{risk budget}$; the utilisation series
$N_t|\ES_{99\%,1\text{d}}|/\text{budget}$ is tracked, and the limit framework is
itself backtested (how often would the chosen limit have bound, and do the
breaches line up with large realized losses).

\paragraph{P\&L attribution.} Decompose simulated daily P\&L into drift /
diffusion / jump / residual and run the FRTB PLA test (Spearman and
Kolmogorov--Smirnov between model and realized P\&L $\to$ green / amber / red).

\paragraph{Hedge.} Minimum-variance and ES-minimising hedge ratios against the
perpetual future; the annualised funding cost of the hedge against the reduction
in $\ES$ and capital; the spot--perp basis risk in the stress sub-periods.

\intuition{The winning model's ES is turned into three numbers a desk actually
uses: how much capital to hold, how large a position the risk budget allows, and
whether a perp hedge is worth its funding cost. The gap between the best and
worst surviving model becomes an explicit model-risk charge rather than a
footnote.}

\section{What the mathematics does not fix}\label{sec:limits}

The first three limitations are properties of the crypto data, not
implementation errors, and the FZ0/MCS machinery is precisely what surfaces
them; the fourth is a scope choice.

\paragraph{(i) MS-GARCH regime identification.} As the two regimes' parameters
$(\omega_k,\alpha_k,\beta_k,\nu_k)$ approach each other the mixture collapses and
the model is unidentified; the Fisher information becomes near-singular and the
log-likelihood has a ridge. On a $500$-day window the sample lies in a
neighbourhood of that non-identified set, so the Hamilton filter's posterior
$\pi_{k,t\mid t}$ stays close to the ergodic distribution regardless of $r_t$.
Empirically, $\operatorname{corr}(\pi_{k^\star,t\mid t}, |r_t|)\approx 0.07$ out
of sample versus $\approx 0.70$ for a single full-sample fit. The VaR from the
predictive mixture is still competitive (the mixture density is robust even when
the weights are muddy); it is the \emph{real-time regime signal} that does not
survive. We therefore report the descriptive regime curve from a full-sample fit
and exclude it from the backtest.

\paragraph{(ii) HAR for VaR.} HAR-type models target
$\E[\RV_{t+1}\mid\Fcal_t]$, the conditional \emph{mean} of realized variance.
The VaR needs a lower quantile of $r_{t+1}$. Even with the mean of $\RV$ correct,
$\RV_{t+1}$ is right-skewed, so its conditional median (and hence the volatility
that matters on a tail day) is above the point forecast; the models therefore
run systematically thin for VaR. This shows up as slight under-coverage for
HAR-RV and HARQ at both $\alpha$ levels.

\paragraph{(iii) EWMA and EGARCH tails.} EWMA's Gaussian innovation has excess
kurtosis zero. EGARCH-$t$ estimates $\nu$, but the log-variance recursion
already absorbs much of the unconditional excess kurtosis, leaving standardized
residuals close to normal, so $\hat\nu$ tends to be large and the $1\%$ quantile
too shallow. Both are visible as breach rates well above $\alpha$ at
$\alpha = 0.01$.

\paragraph{(iv) Estimation risk is bounded, not propagated.} The daily VaR/ES
use the parameter point estimate $\hat\theta$, so the reported one-day interval
is conditional on $\hat\theta=\theta$. The sampling distribution of $\hat\theta$
would widen it (Christoffersen \& Gon\c{c}alves, 2005). We bound the effect on
the \emph{final} estimation window for three archetypes --- Historical
Simulation (a stationary block bootstrap of the window), GARCH-$t$ (draw
$\theta^\star\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat\theta,\widehat{\Sigma}_{\hat\theta})$ from the fitted
asymptotic covariance and re-forecast $\sigma_{t+1}$ without refitting), and
Filtered HS (a parameter draw for the volatility path plus a residual
resample). The prudent ($5$th-percentile) ES draw feeds an
estimation-risk capital add-on, $\mathrm{Cap}(\mathrm{ES}^{\mathrm{prudent}})
-\mathrm{Cap}(\widehat{\mathrm{ES}})$, reported next to the model-risk add-on in
\S7. On this sample it is \$32k--\$73k, an order of magnitude below the
model-risk term: real, but second-order at the decision layer. It is not
carried through every day of every model.

None of (i)--(iii) are bugs. They are what the comparative study is for: to say,
with a Model Confidence Set rather than a narrative, which models one can
actually stand behind.
